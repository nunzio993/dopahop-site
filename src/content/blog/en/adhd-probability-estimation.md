**ADHD and probability estimation** trip each other up more than most people notice. You tell yourself an errand will take fifteen minutes and it eats ninety. You're sure the parcel will arrive before you leave, so you don't plan around it. You wave off a small recurring cost because "it's nothing," and three months later it's very much something. None of this is carelessness or bad math. It's that weighing *how likely* an outcome is — and *how long* something will really take — leans on exactly the brain systems that ADHD strains: working memory, a sense of time, and the pull of the immediate over the distant. This article unpacks why your estimates run off, where it bites in daily life, and gentle ways to check your guesses without becoming a spreadsheet person.

## What "probability estimation" really means day to day

Probability estimation is just the everyday skill of guessing how likely something is and acting on that guess. You do it constantly without naming it: *Will I make the train? Can I afford this? How long will this take? Is this risky?* You rarely compute odds — you feel them, fast, from memory and gut.

That fast feel is where ADHD shows up. To weigh an outcome well, your brain has to hold several things at once (what usually happens, what's different this time, what could go wrong), project them forward in time, and resist the loud, immediate option in favour of the quieter, future one. Each of those is a documented pressure point in ADHD. So the estimate doesn't fail because you didn't try — it fails because the raw materials are harder to assemble.

## Why ADHD makes the future hard to weigh

Three well-studied mechanisms stack up here.

**Time gets compressed and distorted.** Barkley's influential model (Barkley, *Psychological Bulletin*, 1997) describes ADHD partly as a difficulty regulating behaviour *across time* — a "temporal myopia" where the near is vivid and the far is blurry, sorted into a rough "now / not-now." If the future is blurry, any estimate that depends on it (how long, how likely, how soon) is built on shaky ground.

**The future gets discounted steeply.** A reward or consequence that sits later feels smaller than it is. The meta-analysis by Jackson and MacKillop (*Biological Psychiatry: CNI*, 2016) found that people with ADHD discount delayed rewards more steeply than controls, with a medium effect (Cohen's d = 0.43) that held across ages and settings. Marx and colleagues (*Journal of Attention Disorders*, 2021) found a parallel pull: a stronger-than-usual aversion to delay nudges the choice toward the small, immediate option. In estimation terms, a low-probability future cost (the late fee, the missed deadline) barely registers next to the immediate payoff of doing the easy thing now.

**Working memory drops the variables.** Holding "usual time + today's traffic + the thing that always goes wrong" in mind long enough to combine them is real cognitive load. The adult meta-analysis by Alderson and colleagues (*Neuropsychology*, 2013) found working-memory deficits in adults with ADHD, most marked when you have to actively *manipulate* information rather than just store it — which is exactly what a good estimate requires. So the brain reaches for the simplest input it can hold: the best-case version.

That best-case reflex has a famous name in decision psychology: the **planning fallacy** — the well-documented human tendency, described by Kahneman and Tversky, to systematically underestimate how long a task will take and how much it'll cost, even when past experience says otherwise. Everyone does it. ADHD just turns up the volume, because the corrective signals (a clear sense of time, an easily recalled track record) are the ones running quiet.

## Where the off estimates actually bite

This isn't abstract. It lands in specific, repeating places.

- **Deadlines.** You estimate from the version of the task with no interruptions, no setup, no "oh, I need that file." So you start late and finish later — not from laziness but from a sincere, optimistic guess.
- **Money.** A €4 subscription reads as "basically free" because the brain weighs the immediate (tiny) over the cumulative (real). Twelve of those is a quiet leak. We dug into this pattern in [ADHD and Personal Finance: Impulsive Spending and Debt](/en/blog/adhd-personal-finance-impulsivity-debt/).
- **Risk reads that swing.** The same person can be over-optimistic ("it'll be fine, I'll remember") and over-pessimistic ("the whole thing will collapse") within an hour. Poorly *calibrated*, not simply reckless — the estimate isn't tethered to base rates.
- **Decisions made on the loud signal.** The vivid, immediate factor (this feels exciting / this feels scary right now) crowds out the low-probability-but-important one. This is the same engine behind [ADHD impulsive decision-making](/en/blog/adhd-impulsive-decision-making/): the future option is real, just devalued.

The thread through all of these: it's a *calibration* problem, not a character problem. Your estimates aren't random — they're systematically pulled toward the immediate and the optimistic. And systematic errors are the kind you can correct with structure.

## What doesn't work (even though it's the standard advice)

Before the countermeasures, the things that reliably fail for ADHD brains:

- **"Just be more realistic."** Realism isn't a setting you flip. The optimistic estimate *feels* realistic from the inside — that's the whole problem.
- **"Add a buffer in your head."** Mental buffers evaporate. If the padding only lives in your head, working memory drops it the moment something else competes.
- **"Think harder before deciding."** More rumination on a blurry future mostly produces more anxiety, not a sharper estimate. The fix isn't *more* thinking; it's *externalising* the thinking.

## Gentle countermeasures that actually help

The principle behind all of these: stop asking your in-the-moment brain to be the calculator. Move the work outside your head, where it can't be quietly discounted or forgotten.

### 1. Use written track records instead of fresh guesses

The single best correction for the planning fallacy is the **outside view**: instead of imagining how long *this* task will take, look at how long *similar tasks actually took you* last time. Don't trust the memory — write it down. Jot the real duration of recurring tasks for a week. "Email the landlord" that you swore was five minutes? It was twenty. Next time, you estimate from the record, not the fantasy. The number is on paper, so it can't be talked down.

### 2. Run a tiny pre-mortem

Before committing to a plan, spend sixty seconds on one question: *"If this goes wrong, what's the most likely reason?"* This is a pre-mortem, and it works because it forces the low-probability-but-real failure modes into view *before* the immediate excitement buries them. You're not being negative — you're giving the quiet signal a moment at the microphone. Usually one obvious risk pops up ("I won't have the file ready"), and you can plan around it cheaply.

### 3. Anchor to base rates, not vibes

For anything recurring, find the **base rate** — the boring average — and start there. How often does the train actually run late? How much did last quarter's "small" purchases add up to? Generic research on planning is clear that starting from a reference number and adjusting beats inventing a fresh guess every time. The base rate is your anchor; today's optimism only gets to nudge it, not replace it.

### 4. Make the implementation concrete

Vague intentions ("I'll deal with it later") leave the future undefined, which is exactly where ADHD estimates dissolve. **Implementation intentions** — the "when X happens, I'll do Y" format — convert a fuzzy plan into a triggered action. The meta-analysis by Gollwitzer and Sheeran (*Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*, 2006) found a medium-to-large effect (d = .65) on follow-through, with hard goals completed about three times more often. "I'll start the report sometime" becomes "when I sit down after lunch, I open the document and write one line."

### 5. Break the task down so the estimate is small

A big task is impossible to estimate accurately — too many hidden steps. A small one is easy. Splitting work into concrete steps doesn't just make starting easier; it makes each piece *estimable*, which quietly fixes the planning fallacy from the other end. ## Frequently asked questions

### Does ADHD make you bad at math or statistics?

No. This isn't about numerical ability. It's about *real-time* estimation under load — guessing how long, how likely, how risky while time feels blurry and the immediate option shouts. Plenty of people with ADHD are excellent at formal maths and still misjudge whether they'll make the 9:15.

### Why am I sometimes over-optimistic and sometimes catastrophising?

Because the estimate isn't well *calibrated* — it's pulled by whatever signal is loudest right now, not anchored to a stable base rate. Excitement pulls it optimistic; anxiety pulls it catastrophic. Anchoring to a written track record or an average steadies the swing.

### Is the planning fallacy an ADHD thing?

The planning fallacy is a universal human bias, described in mainstream decision psychology — everyone underestimates task time. ADHD doesn't cause it, but documented traits like distorted time perception and steep delay discounting tend to amplify it, so the gap between estimate and reality runs wider.

### What's the single most useful habit?

Writing down how long recurring tasks *actually* took. One week of honest durations gives you real numbers to estimate from, and reading them back is a gentle, shame-free reality check — no willpower required.
