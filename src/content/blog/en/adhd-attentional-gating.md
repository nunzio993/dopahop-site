**ADHD and attentional gating** explains a lot of what gets mislabelled as "being easily distracted." Attentional gating is your brain's filter: the system that decides which stimuli get through to your conscious attention and which get held at the door. When you have ADHD and you're trying to read one paragraph, but the hum of the fridge, a colleague's phone call two desks over, and a notification you haven't even looked at yet all elbow their way into your head at once — that isn't a discipline problem. It's a filter that's letting too much in. In this article we'll look at what attentional gating actually is, why it runs leakier in ADHD, why "just focus harder" is useless advice, and what genuinely helps: not white-knuckling your attention, but rebuilding the filter on the outside — in your environment, not your willpower.

## What attentional gating actually is

**Attentional gating** is the brain's process of letting relevant information through and damping down the irrelevant. Concretely: right now, you're probably not noticing the feeling of your socks, the background noise around you, or the edge of the screen — until you read that sentence and suddenly do. That selective spotlight is gating at work. A healthy filter keeps the irrelevant stuff in the background so the thing you care about can stay in the foreground.

This filtering isn't one switch. It's a constant, automatic negotiation between *bottom-up* signals (a sudden noise, a flashing light, movement in your peripheral vision — stimuli that grab attention on their own) and *top-down* control (your intention to keep reading this sentence and ignore the noise). When the system works well, top-down goals win most of the time. Background stays background.

The point is that filtering is not the same as focusing. Focus is what you do with the thing in the foreground. Gating is the quieter, upstream job of keeping everything else out of the foreground in the first place. You can have perfectly good focus and still struggle, because the door behind you keeps swinging open.

## Why the filter runs leakier in ADHD

In ADHD, that door doesn't seal as well. The same fridge hum your neurotypical colleague genuinely doesn't register stays *audible* to you — not because your hearing is sharper, but because the gate that should have demoted it to "ignore" didn't fully close. More gets through, so more competes for the limited space in your attention.

This lines up with what the broader research on ADHD attention shows. A meta-analysis of adult ADHD neuropsychology by Hervey, Epstein and Curry (2004, *Neuropsychology*) found deficits clustered in specific domains — attention and behavioural inhibition among them — while basic processes like simple reaction time stayed normal. The impairment is *selective*, not global: the machinery for noticing is fine, but the machinery for suppressing the irrelevant and inhibiting the off-task is where things get expensive.

Willcutt and colleagues (2005, *Biological Psychiatry*), in a large meta-analysis of executive function in ADHD, similarly found the strongest effects on response inhibition and sustained attention (vigilance). Inhibition is, at its core, the ability to *not* respond to something. A leakier filter and weaker inhibition are two views of the same underlying difficulty: things that should have been kept out get in, and once they're in, they're hard to dismiss.

A quick, important caveat. "Sensory gating" and "attentional gating" are precise neuroscience terms with their own specialised literature, and the everyday distractibility most adults with ADHD describe is a broader, looser thing than any single lab measure. So treat the filter as a useful *metaphor* grounded in documented attention and inhibition findings — not as a claim that one specific brain mechanism has been pinned down for you personally. The research is clearest on the consequences: more competing input, harder suppression, a smaller margin for error.

This is also why ADHD distractibility and sensory sensitivity so often travel together. If background noise, scratchy clothing labels, bright overhead lights or a busy visual field all leak through more easily, the world simply feels louder. That's not fragility. It's a filter doing less filtering.

## Why "just focus harder" doesn't work

Here's the trap. If the problem were focus, then trying harder might help. But the problem is upstream of focus — it's the gate. And you can't consciously will a gate shut that operates automatically, any more than you can will yourself to stop hearing a sound once you've noticed it.

Worse, "focus harder" actively costs you. Every time an irrelevant stimulus gets through, you spend effort dragging your attention back. Do that a few hundred times an hour and you're not lazy by lunchtime — you're depleted. The recovery isn't free, either: a well-known study by Mark, Gudith and Klocke (2008, at the CHI conference) found that after an interruption, people took on average around 23 minutes to get back to their original depth of concentration. That's in the *general* population. Stack a leaky filter on top, and you're being interrupted far more often, paying that re-entry cost again and again.

So the people white-knuckling their way through an open-plan office, exhausted and ashamed they "can't concentrate like everyone else," aren't failing at effort. They're trying to solve an environmental problem with a willpower tool. It doesn't scale, and it isn't your fault that it doesn't.

The reframe that actually helps: stop trying to strengthen the filter from the inside. **Move it outside.**

## What works: externalise the filter

If your internal gate is leaky, the fix is to reduce what reaches it in the first place — and to put the filtering into your environment, your tools, and your routines instead of your tired prefrontal cortex. Think of it as building the filter you don't reliably have on the inside.

### 1. Cut the input at the source

The cheapest distraction to handle is the one that never arrives. Before reaching for a coping strategy, remove the stimulus:

- **Headphones, every time.** Noise-cancelling headphones or earplugs aren't a luxury here — they're a literal external gate for sound. Even silence-with-padding beats an open room. If silence feels too sterile, a steady, featureless sound (rain, brown noise) gives the filter one predictable thing to dismiss instead of dozens of unpredictable ones.
- **Kill notifications, don't just ignore them.** A banner you "decide not to check" has already cost you — it got through the gate. Silence them at the system level, not your self-control level. Your phone face-down in another room is doing filtering your brain doesn't have to.
- **Clear the visual field.** A busy desk is a stream of small bottom-up grabs all day. You don't need a minimalist shrine; you need the immediate area in front of you to hold only what the current task needs.

### 2. Lower the overall stimulation, not just the obvious noise

Sometimes no single distractor is loud — there's just *too much at once*. A bright, echoey, busy space taxes a leaky filter even when nothing's wrong. Where you can, choose lower-stimulation conditions: a corner instead of the centre of the room, your back to the wall instead of to foot traffic, dimmer warm light instead of harsh overhead glare. If the input is overwhelming the gate, this can tip into outright [cognitive overload](/en/blog/adhd-cognitive-overload/) — the point where the brain stops triaging and simply stalls. Reducing ambient stimulation buys back margin before you hit that wall.

### 3. Make the off-task pull lose

A leaky filter is worst when there's something more rewarding one click away. The notification only wins because the document is boring and the feed is not. You can't out-discipline that gap, but you can shrink it: remove the competing app from the phone, log out of the tempting site, leave your phone in another room during a focused block. You're not testing your willpower — you're making the wrong choice take more steps than the right one.

### 4. Catch the thoughts that leak from the inside

Not every distraction comes from outside. Half of ADHD distractibility is your own brain throwing up "oh, I should email Mom" mid-task. Trying to hold that thought *and* keep working overloads an already-stretched system. The move is to get it out of your head fast so the filter has one less thing to juggle — which is exactly what a [brain dump](/#cosa-fa) is for: ten seconds to park the thought somewhere safe, then back to the task without the nag.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is being easily distracted always a sign of ADHD?

No. Everyone's filter leaks sometimes — when you're tired, stressed, hungry or under-slept. Distractibility becomes a clinical concern only when it's persistent, shows up across multiple settings, started in childhood, and genuinely interferes with daily life. A single distractible afternoon is just being human. If the pattern is lifelong and costly, that's a conversation for a professional, not a self-diagnosis from a blog.

### Why am I so sensitive to noise and light if I have ADHD?

Many adults with ADHD report heightened sensitivity to background noise, bright light and other sensory input. A useful way to understand it: if the filter that normally demotes irrelevant stimuli is leakier, more of that input reaches conscious attention, so the world feels louder and busier. It's a real, common experience — not over-reacting — though sensory sensitivity also shows up in other conditions, so it isn't proof of ADHD on its own.

### Do noise-cancelling headphones actually help, or is it a placebo?

For most people whose attention leaks toward sound, removing or masking that sound removes the distraction at the source — which is more reliable than trying to ignore it. There's no universal rule, and total silence overstimulates some people while soothing others. The honest answer is to test it for a week and keep what works for your brain, not what works in someone else's productivity video.

### Isn't reducing distractions just avoiding the problem?

It's the opposite. "Focus harder" tries to fix a leaky filter with willpower, which depletes fast. Externalising the filter — controlling the environment, cutting input, offloading thoughts — works *with* how the ADHD brain is built instead of against it. Using accommodations isn't a moral failure or a crutch; it's the strategy the evidence on attention and inhibition actually points toward.

### What's the difference between focus and attentional gating?

Focus is sustaining attention on the thing in the foreground. Gating is the upstream job of keeping everything else *out* of the foreground. You can have decent focus and still struggle, because the gating is what keeps failing — which is why working on your environment (gating) often helps more than trying to "focus harder" (focus).
