**ADHD and debugging** is a strange relationship. You've been there: it's 1 a.m., you've been hunting one bug for four hours, you've tried the same fix six slightly different ways, and you cannot physically make yourself close the laptop. Or the opposite — twenty minutes in, the stack trace makes no sense, a wave of frustration hits, and you quit. Not just the bug. The whole project. For three weeks. Neither of those is laziness or stubbornness in the moral sense. They're two faces of the same thing: an ADHD brain that struggles to regulate *how much* attention to put on a problem, and *when* to let go. This article is about spotting which mode you're in — and giving yourself simple rules for when to keep going and when to walk away.

This isn't only about code. Debugging is just the cleanest example of "tackling a problem that fights back." A tax form, a leaking tap, a spreadsheet formula, an argument you keep replaying — same two failure modes, same fix.

## The two failure modes: stubborn persistence and premature abandonment

Most problem-solving advice assumes you have one dial: *try harder*. ADHD brains have two dials, and they tend to swing to the extremes.

**Stubborn persistence.** You lock onto the problem and can't disengage, even when it's clearly time to. This is hyperfocus wearing a work badge. Hyperfocus has been described in the research as intense engagement and sustained attention on one task, with reduced awareness of everything else — including the fact that you skipped lunch and the fix stopped making sense an hour ago (Ashinoff & Abu-Akel, *Psychological Research*, 2021). It feels productive. Sometimes it is. But past a point, you're not solving the bug — you're stuck in a loop, retyping the same idea and hoping reality changes.

**Premature abandonment.** The flip side. Frustration spikes, the problem stops giving you any feedback or small wins, boredom creeps in, and the brain that was so locked-in an hour ago now wants *anything else*. You don't just pause — you bail. ADHD comes with stronger, faster emotional reactions for many adults, and frustration is one of the hardest to ride out. The bug doesn't just feel annoying; it feels like proof you're not good enough, and quitting makes that feeling stop.

Here's the uncomfortable part: **both modes can hit the same person on the same day.** You over-persist on a bug you should drop, then under-persist on the boring-but-fixable one right after. The skill isn't "more grit" or "more chill." It's recognising which dial is stuck.

## How to tell which mode you're actually in

You can't apply the right rule until you know which way you're broken *right now*. A quick self-check, mid-problem:

- **Am I generating new ideas, or repeating old ones?** Persistence is healthy while you're still trying genuinely different approaches. The moment you're re-running the same fix with tiny variations, you've crossed into the stuck loop.
- **What's my body doing?** Clenched jaw, held breath, leaning into the screen, snapping at people — that's frustration driving, not problem-solving. Note it.
- **When did I last get a small win?** Problems give feedback: an error message changes, a test goes green, you rule something out. If you've had zero feedback for a long stretch, the brain's reward system is running on empty — and that's exactly when both over-persistence (chasing the hit) and abandonment (giving up on the hit) get triggered.
- **Could I explain out loud why I'm still on this?** If the honest answer is "because I've already spent so long on it," that's sunk cost talking — a well-documented thinking trap where past effort, which you can't get back, keeps you committed to something that isn't working.

Naming the mode out loud ("okay, I'm in the stubborn loop") is half the battle. It moves the problem from "I am failing" to "my attention is doing its thing," which is much easier to act on without shame.

## Rules for when to persist (and when to step away)

You don't want to make this decision with raw willpower in the heat of the moment — that's the one resource ADHD doesn't reliably have when frustrated. You want *rules you set in advance*, so present-you just follows them.

### The two-attempts rule

Pick a problem. Give yourself **two genuinely different attempts**. Not two variations of the same idea — two actually different angles. If both fail, that's your signal to do one of three things: **ask** someone, **search** properly, or **break**. The rule converts a vague "am I stuck?" into a countable trigger. Two strikes, then change something. This protects you from the stubborn loop, where attempt number fourteen is just attempt number two in a different font.

### Timeboxing the hard part

Set a timer before you start the hairy bit — twenty-five minutes is a natural unit. When it goes off, you don't have to stop, but you *have to look up* and ask: new ideas, or old ones? Feedback, or silence? The timer is an external checkpoint that your hyperfocus can't quietly delete. The point isn't to cut focus short. It's to give you a moment of altitude before you sink back in.

### The rubber duck (yes, really)

The "rubber duck" method comes from programming culture: you explain your problem, line by line, out loud, to an inanimate object — a rubber duck on your desk. Astonishingly often, you find the bug mid-sentence, because forcing the problem into words exposes the assumption you'd been skating over. For ADHD brains this does double duty: it externalises your working memory (so you're not juggling the whole problem in your head), and it interrupts the emotional loop. You can't be quietly seething *and* narrating at the same time. No duck? A text-to-yourself, a notes app, or a patient friend works too.

### The "step away, on purpose" move

Walking away is a tool, not a failure — *if you do it deliberately and leave a breadcrumb*. The danger of ADHD abandonment isn't the break; it's that you leave with no thread back in, so returning costs more than starting fresh, and you never return. So before you step away: write one line of where you are and what you'd try next. Then go. A real break — a walk, water, something physical — lets the background part of your brain keep chewing, and solutions genuinely do surface in the shower. The breadcrumb is what turns "abandoned" into "paused." A quick [brain dump](/#cosa-fa) is perfect for that one line — ten seconds, out of your head, waiting for you when you come back.

## Why ADHD makes this harder (and why it's not your fault)

A bit of mechanism, because understanding it takes the sting out.

Hyperfocus isn't the same as flow, even though they feel related. Flow is the smooth, voluntary absorption described by Csikszentmihalyi (*Beyond Boredom and Anxiety*, 1975) — you choose it, it's matched to your skill, and you come out refreshed. ADHD hyperfocus tends to be *involuntary*: it grabs you, it's hard to steer or stop, and it often ends in a crash rather than a glow. Research using the Hyperfocusing Scale found that adults with higher ADHD symptoms score significantly higher on hyperfocus tendencies than controls (Ozel-Kizil et al., *Research in Developmental Disabilities*, 2016). So the "can't put the bug down" experience isn't a character flaw — it's a measurable feature of how your attention regulates.

The abandonment side leans on reward and frustration. A problem that stops giving small wins stops feeding the brain's reward system, and for ADHD that drop is felt sharply. Combine low feedback with quick-firing frustration and you get the urge to escape — strongly, suddenly, and often before the problem was actually unsolvable. None of this means you're undisciplined. It means your "keep going" and "let go" signals are noisier than average. Rules and timers aren't crutches; they're the external scaffolding that does the regulating your brain finds expensive to do internally. ## Frequently asked questions

### Is hyperfocus on a bug a good thing or a bad thing?

Both, depending on timing. Early on, intense focus is exactly what hard problems need. The trouble is the *no off switch* part — staying locked in long after you've stopped making progress. The fix isn't to suppress hyperfocus; it's to add external checkpoints (a timer, the two-attempts rule) so you notice when useful focus has become a stuck loop.

### How do I know if I'm quitting too soon or quitting wisely?

Quitting wisely means you tried genuinely different approaches, you've left a note on where you are, and you're stepping away to let your brain process — planning to return. Quitting too soon usually rides on a frustration spike, leaves no thread back, and abandons the whole thing, not just the moment. The breadcrumb is the tell: a deliberate pause leaves one; an emotional bail leaves none.

### Does the rubber duck method actually work, or is it a gimmick?

It genuinely works, and not just for code. Saying a problem out loud, step by step, forces your brain to surface the assumption it was skipping over silently. For ADHD it has a bonus: it offloads working memory and breaks the emotional loop. The "duck" can be anything — a notes app, a voice memo, a willing human.

### What if two attempts plus a break still don't solve it?

Then the honest move is to ask for help or accept the problem is bigger than a solo session — and that's a normal, professional outcome, not a personal failure. Some bugs need a second pair of eyes, fresh documentation, or a night's sleep. The two-attempts rule isn't there to solve everything alone; it's there to stop you burning hours before you reach for the obvious next step.
