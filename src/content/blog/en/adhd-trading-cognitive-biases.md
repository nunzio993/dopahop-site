**ADHD and trading biases** belong in the same sentence more often than the finance world likes to admit. Every trader, neurotypical or not, fights the same set of mental shortcuts: loss aversion, FOMO, recency bias, the sunk-cost fallacy, overconfidence, confirmation bias. They come from how human brains evaluate risk and reward — Kahneman and Tversky spent careers mapping them. The difference with ADHD isn't that you have *different* biases. It's that the traits sitting underneath ADHD — impulsivity, weak inhibitory control, a hunger for stimulation, intense emotions, a working memory that drops things — act like an amplifier on each one. In this article we'll go bias by bias, show exactly which ADHD trait turns the dial up, and give you one concrete countermeasure for each. If you've already read [ADHD and Overtrading](/en/blog/adhd-overtrading/), think of this as the close-up: not *why* you trade too much, but *which specific mental traps* are doing the damage.

## What a cognitive bias actually is (and why ADHD changes the volume)

A cognitive bias is a systematic shortcut your brain takes when deciding under uncertainty — useful most of the time, predictably wrong in specific situations. In trading, these shortcuts cost real money because markets are uncertainty machines.

ADHD doesn't invent new biases. It changes the *gain* on the ones everybody has. Research on adults with ADHD points to weaker behavioural inhibition and reward systems that respond differently to immediate versus delayed payoffs — the meta-analysis by Hervey, Epstein and Curry (2004, *Neuropsychology*) found deficits in behavioural inhibition and working memory as central, selective features in adult ADHD, not a global slowdown. When the underlying brake is lighter and the pull toward *now* is heavier, a bias that nudges a neurotypical trader can shove an ADHD trader.

Here's the practical version, bias by bias.

## Loss aversion and the sunk-cost fallacy: holding the bag

**Loss aversion** is the well-documented finding that losing 100 hurts roughly twice as much as gaining 100 feels good. The **sunk-cost fallacy** is its cousin: refusing to exit a losing position because you've "already put so much into it."

For an ADHD brain these two are turbocharged by emotional intensity and rejection sensitivity. A loss isn't just a number on a screen — it can land as a hit to your sense of competence, the kind of sharp emotional sting many ADHD adults describe. Closing the trade means *admitting* the loss, making it real and final. So you hold, hoping it comes back, telling yourself it's "not a loss until I sell." Weak inhibitory control means the rational exit plan you wrote loses to the in-the-moment urge to avoid the pain.

**Countermeasure: pre-commit the exit before you enter.** Set the stop-loss at the moment you open the position, when you're calm, and make it automatic so the platform closes it without you. Decisions you've already made don't need willpower in the heat of the moment — and willpower in the heat of the moment is exactly what an ADHD brain has least of.

## FOMO and recency bias: chasing the thing that just moved

**FOMO** (fear of missing out) is the urge to jump into something already running because everyone else seems to be winning. **Recency bias** is overweighting what just happened — assuming the last three green candles predict the next three.

This pair is where ADHD traits hit hardest. Stimulation-seeking makes a fast-moving asset genuinely *exciting* in a way a flat, boring, sensible position never will. Impulsivity collapses the gap between "that looks interesting" and "I've bought it." And the research on delay processing is relevant here: the meta-analysis by Jackson and MacKillop (2016, *Biological Psychiatry: CNNI*) found that adults and others with ADHD discount delayed rewards more steeply (Cohen's d = 0.43) — the slow, patient payoff is worth less to your brain than the thing flashing *right now*. A pump you can ride *today* beats a strategy that pays off in a quarter.

**Countermeasure: add a forced delay between impulse and order.** A simple rule — "I do not buy anything I first heard about less than 24 hours ago" — converts a recency-driven impulse into a decision your slower, calmer self gets to veto. The delay is the whole point: it gives your weak brake time to engage.

## Overconfidence and the dopamine of a winning streak

**Overconfidence bias** is overestimating how much you know and how much control you have over outcomes. After a few wins it inflates fast — and a winning trade is a potent hit of dopamine.

ADHD reward systems respond differently to that hit. The meta-analytic fMRI review by Plichta and Scheres (2014, *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*) describes altered ventral-striatal responsiveness to reward anticipation in ADHD. In plain terms, the brain's reward circuitry is wired unusually around anticipating payoffs — which can make the *chase* of the next win feel disproportionately compelling. A streak doesn't just make you feel smart; it cranks up the appetite for the next bet, and overconfidence supplies the story ("I've cracked it") that justifies sizing up right when you should be sizing down.

**Countermeasure: fix position size by rule, not by mood.** Decide your maximum risk per trade as a fixed percentage and never raise it because you're "hot." A rule that's blind to your current confidence protects you from the version of you that a three-win streak creates.

## Confirmation bias and the working-memory leak

**Confirmation bias** is seeking out information that agrees with the position you already hold and quietly ignoring the rest. You bought the stock, so you read the bullish thread and skim past the bearish one.

ADHD adds two specific failure modes. First, working memory: meta-analytic work by Alderson and colleagues (2013, *Neuropsychology*) found a moderate working-memory deficit in adults with ADHD, strongest when information has to be actively held and manipulated. The disconfirming evidence you read yesterday simply doesn't stay loaded in your head while you're staring at the chart today, so the bullish story wins by default — it's the one that's present. Second, the emotional cost of being wrong (again, that rejection-sensitivity sting) makes disconfirming information actively unpleasant to look at, so attention slides off it.

**Countermeasure: write the bear case down, externally.** Before entering, note in one line *what would prove this trade wrong* and keep it visible next to the position. Externalising it fixes the working-memory leak — the disconfirming evidence can't quietly evaporate if it's written where you have to see it.

## A quick map: trait to bias

If you only remember one thing, remember which lever pulls which trap:

- **Impulsivity / weak inhibition** → FOMO, impulsive entries, blown stop-losses
- **Stimulation-seeking** → chasing volatile movers, recency bias, boredom-trading flat markets
- **Steep delay discounting** → preferring fast payoffs over patient strategy
- **Intense emotions / rejection sensitivity** → loss aversion, sunk-cost holding
- **Reward-system differences** → overconfidence on streaks, appetite for the next hit
- **Limited working memory** → confirmation bias, losing track of the plan

The biases are universal. The amplifier is the ADHD-specific part — and it's also the part you can build scaffolding around.

## In summary

You don't have a *unique* trading problem. You have the same six biases as everyone else, running through a brain that turns the volume up on each one. That reframe matters: it means the fixes aren't about becoming a more disciplined person through sheer effort — an approach that fails ADHD brains over and over. The fixes are structural. Pre-committed exits beat in-the-moment willpower. A forced delay beats a fast impulse. A fixed position size beats a confident mood. A written bear case beats a leaky memory.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does ADHD make me a worse trader?

Not inherently. ADHD amplifies the cognitive biases every trader faces, which raises the risk of impulsive, emotionally driven decisions. But the same traits — fast pattern recognition, comfort with uncertainty — can be neutral or even useful inside a system with strong external guardrails. The risk lives in unstructured, high-stimulation trading, not in your brain being "wrong."

### Are these cognitive biases an ADHD thing or a human thing?

Human. Loss aversion, FOMO, recency, sunk-cost, overconfidence and confirmation bias are documented across the general population — they come from behavioural finance, not from ADHD. What's ADHD-specific is the *intensity*: impulsivity, weak inhibition, stimulation-seeking and a steeper preference for immediate rewards turn the gain up on each one.

### What's the single most useful countermeasure?

For most ADHD traders, pre-committing the exit (an automatic stop-loss set at entry) does the heaviest lifting, because it removes the in-the-moment decision that ADHD makes hardest. If you add only one rule, start there.

### Can an app fix this?

No app fixes a cognitive bias. Tools can only support the behaviour around it — parking an impulse, noticing a bad-mood window, externalising a plan so your working memory doesn't drop it. The decisions, and any professional advice, stay with you and the people qualified to give it.
