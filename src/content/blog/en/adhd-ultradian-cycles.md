**ADHD and ultradian cycles** explain something you've probably blamed yourself for a hundred times. You sit down at 9 a.m. sharp, you're flying, the ideas land, and then around 10:30 the floor drops out — suddenly you're re-reading the same sentence, checking your phone, getting up for a third coffee you don't want. You didn't "lose focus" because you're undisciplined. Your brain rode up a wave of alertness and then rode back down, the way it's supposed to. **Ultradian rhythms** are roughly 90-to-120-minute cycles of energy and alertness that repeat across the whole day — and when you have ADHD, the peaks and troughs tend to feel more extreme. In this article: what these cycles are, why ADHD makes them louder, why "just push through" backfires, and how to work *with* the waves instead of shaming yourself during the dips.

## What ultradian cycles actually are

An ultradian rhythm is any biological cycle that repeats more than once a day. The one that matters for focus is the **Basic Rest-Activity Cycle (BRAC)** — a roughly 90-to-120-minute swing between higher and lower alertness that the sleep researcher Nathaniel Kleitman first described in the early 1960s. He noticed it in sleep stages, then realized the same wave keeps rolling while you're awake.

In plain terms: your body doesn't run on a flat battery that slowly drains. It runs on a series of waves. For about 90 minutes you climb toward a peak of alertness, then for a stretch you slide into a trough — foggier, restless, hungrier, more easily bored. Then it climbs again.

This is a normal, universal piece of human chronobiology. Everyone has it. You are not broken for getting tired mid-morning. What's different for you is probably how loud the signal feels and how hard the dip lands.

## Why ADHD makes the waves louder

Here's the careful part: ultradian rhythms are general chronobiology, not an ADHD-specific finding. There isn't a clean clinical map saying "the ADHD BRAC is exactly X minutes long." So instead of inventing one, let's connect the dots through mechanisms that *are* well documented in ADHD.

**First: attention in ADHD is more variable, moment to moment.** The classic meta-analytic picture of adult ADHD (Hervey, Epstein and Curry, *Neuropsychology*, 2004) describes deficits across attention and behavioral inhibition that are selective rather than global — your performance isn't uniformly low, it swings. When attention already fluctuates more than average, a normal ultradian dip doesn't just nudge you off course; it can drop you off a cliff.

**Second: the ADHD brain is hungrier for stimulation.** Research on reward processing shows reduced ventral-striatal response when the ADHD brain anticipates a reward (Plichta and Scheres, *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews*, 2014). During an alertness peak, an interesting task generates enough internal "pull" to hold you. During the trough — when the same task suddenly feels flat — that pull collapses faster than it would for a neurotypical person. The dip isn't only about energy; it's about how quickly a task stops feeling rewarding.

**Third: time perception is already distorted.** Research shows people with ADHD struggle to feel time passing accurately. So the dip sneaks up on you. One moment you're deep in, the next you "wake up" forty minutes deep into a tab spiral, with no felt sense of when the wave turned. If you want to go deeper on that, we cover it in [ADHD and Time Management: Why Time Feels Distorted](/en/blog/adhd-time-management-distorted-perception/).

Put those together and you get the lived experience: brilliant peaks, brutal troughs, and very little warning between them.

## Why "just push through" backfires

The standard productivity advice is to ignore the dip and grind. For an ADHD brain, that's close to the worst option. Here's what actually happens when you force output through a trough:

- **You burn disproportionate fuel for almost no result.** Pushing focus uphill during a low costs far more effort than the same work would cost twenty minutes later on the next upswing.
- **You teach yourself that the task is miserable.** When you grind a task during a dip, your brain files it under "this thing feels awful," which makes it harder to start next time — the opposite of what you wanted.
- **You stack the dips.** Skip the rest your body asked for and the next peak comes in lower. Do that all day and you arrive at evening flattened. That repeated pattern is part of how ADHD burnout builds faster than people expect, something we unpack in [ADHD Burnout: Why It Hits Faster and Returns More Often](/en/blog/adhd-burnout-accelerated-dynamics/).

None of this means you're lazy. It means you're trying to run a wave-shaped system on a straight-line schedule, and the schedule is wrong, not you.

## How to work with the waves

The goal isn't to "fix" your rhythm. It's to stop fighting it. A few concrete moves:

### 1. Work in blocks that fit the wave, not the clock

Drop the idea of a heroic four-hour focus marathon. Aim for a single block of meaningful work — somewhere in the 45-to-90-minute range — and then *stop on purpose* before the trough flattens you. A short, structured timer is genuinely useful here: it gives the block a hard edge so you're not relying on a sense of time you don't have. If starting is the hard part, [the Pomodoro timer in DopaHop](/#cosa-fa) launches on its own once you press start — you only have to think about the work, not the clock.

### 2. Take real breaks at the dip signals

The dip has tells: restlessness, snack-seeking, re-reading, an itch to check your phone, a sudden urge to "just quickly" reorganize something. Those aren't failures of willpower — they're your BRAC turning over. Treat them as a *cue*, not a verdict. Step away. Move your body, look out a window, get water. A real break — not scrolling, which keeps your brain switched on — lets the trough pass so the next peak can build.

### 3. Spend your peaks on the work that needs you

You don't get unlimited high-alertness windows in a day, so don't waste them on email and admin. When you feel the climb, that's the time for the hard, interesting, brain-heavy task. Save the low-stakes, low-focus stuff — tidying, sorting, simple replies — for the troughs, when your brain wants something easy and lightly stimulating anyway.

### 4. Stop scheduling against your own biology

If you reliably crater at 2 p.m., stop booking your most demanding task for 2 p.m. and then hating yourself when it doesn't happen. Notice your real pattern over a week or two and arrange the heavy work around your actual peaks. This works best alongside your daily rhythm too — energy waves sit *inside* your larger sleep-wake cycle, which for ADHD is often shifted later (see [ADHD Sleep: Disrupted Circadian Rhythms, Explained](/en/blog/adhd-sleep-circadian-rhythm/)).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is there an "ADHD ultradian rhythm" that's different from everyone else's?

Not in any precise, clinically measured sense. Ultradian rhythms like the Basic Rest-Activity Cycle are universal human chronobiology. What's different in ADHD is how the peaks and troughs *feel* — research documents more variable attention and a quicker drop-off when a task stops being rewarding, which can make the same wave land much harder.

### How long should my work blocks be?

There's no magic number. Many people find something in the 45-to-90-minute range fits the rise of a single ultradian wave. The more useful habit is to stop at the first clear dip signals rather than to hit an exact time. Experiment for a week and notice what your own wave does.

### What counts as a "real" break?

Anything that lets your brain downshift: moving, stretching, stepping outside, water, staring out a window. Scrolling a feed usually doesn't count — it keeps the brain in a switched-on, reward-seeking state, so the trough never fully clears and the next peak comes in weaker.

### What if I can't find any peaks at all?

Some days are flat, and stretches of life are flat — that can overlap with low sleep, stress, or low mood. If the flatness is constant for weeks, it's worth talking to your GP (UK) or primary care provider (US), who can refer you to a psychiatrist. Persistent loss of energy and interest is worth a real conversation, not a productivity hack.
